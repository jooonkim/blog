**How I use AI agents for customer work while keeping the facts under review**

## Why this matters

AI agents can now read your email, query live data, and draft pitch decks from end to end. Speed is the most obvious use of these capabilities in customer-facing work. Producing the same meeting recap in two minutes instead of twenty certainly saves time, but we must ask ourselves what else becomes possible.

[Felix Rieseberg](https://felixrieseberg.com/how-to-be-more-ambitious-a-tutorial-for-humans-with-models/) describes three other dimensions of ambition: altitude, range, and depth. In customer work, altitude means looking across the customer's business to understand the outcome they need. Range means drawing on disciplines such as data analysis, computer science, and domain expertise that we might previously have needed another team to provide. Depth means that you can take on questions and tasks that would transform a customer's business that you previously lacked the skills to answer.

That ambition brings complexity. The agent has to combine information from several sources, choose appropriate methods, and explain recommendations that a customer may act on. We need a framework for deciding what it should know, how it should work, and what we need to verify. 

After building and teaching agent workflows with clients and within our sales organization at Google, including a guest lecture at UC Berkeley Haas Executive Education, I propose a **Know, Execute, Prove** framework for customer-facing work:

* **Know:** Keep a small, dated record of what we know about the customer.
* **Execute:** Give the agent written procedures for recurring work.
* **Prove:** Check consequential claims against current, inspectable evidence before a customer sees them.

The three parts depend on one another. A procedure needs customer context; a diagnosis needs live evidence; verified findings should update the customer record.

## Give the agent less to read, and better material

Models can accept long inputs, but a long context window does not guarantee that a buried fact will shape the answer. I have found that stale information is especially troublesome. A May revenue target beside a September target can look like two equally valid instructions. An old report can sound current when its date is missing.

The practical response is to load information as the task requires it. At the start of a customer question, the agent needs a short description of the customer and a way to find the relevant file. It can open the full file when needed. A procedure can similarly load when the task calls for it. Query results should be small enough for the agent, and the seller, to inspect.

This approach is often called progressive disclosure. The research on “lost in the middle” and context rot helps explain why it can work, though performance varies by model and task. I treat context as something to curate: include current material that helps answer the question and leave the rest available for retrieval.

Adding more context often feels like diligence. If we give the agent every document and explain every exception, we hope it will make fewer mistakes. The difficulty is that we also give it more opportunities to use an outdated target, follow a superseded instruction, or mistake an example for a fact.

### What to load for the next task

Suppose you are preparing a renewal review. A familiar prompt might be: “Read these twelve documents, revisit the conversation where we made last quarter's deck, and follow that workflow with all the goals we discussed.” Before running it, decide what the agent needs now:

* Load the current customer file, the renewal question, and the procedure for preparing the review.
* Keep the contract, usage reports, and previous deck available to open when a specific step needs them.
* Move superseded targets and abandoned draft instructions out of the active context. Preserve their sources as history, especially if you need to explain what changed.

The resulting prompt can be: “Prepare a renewal review using the current customer file and our review procedure. Confirm the renewal date in the contract and pull usage for the agreed reporting period. Use last quarter's deck as a layout reference. Flag conflicting goals before making a recommendation.”

After a successful task, save the procedure and final deliverable where the agent can retrieve them. Reconstructing the workflow from a long conversation forces the next run to sift through the failed attempts as well.

### Decide what to trust

I use the following order when preparing customer advice:

1. A live query or primary system record from the current session can support a current metric or status. Save the query, source, and date.
2. The customer's own dated words can support a statement about their goals or decisions. Reconfirm anything that may have changed.
3. Meeting notes and the customer file provide context. Check numerical claims against a current source before citing them.
4. Pasted reports, screenshots, exports, and third-party audits suggest questions to investigate. Record their source and date.
5. Model inference is a hypothesis until another source supports it.

I pass the agent the source thread, document, or query output when I have it. A paraphrase can erase the odd detail that makes a diagnosis possible. I also state the audience, the decision the work is meant to support, and the form of the deliverable. If a fact is uncertain, I mark it as such.

### Keep the setup simple

The agent's working setup consists of its customer files, procedures, tools, and rules for review. I keep instructions short enough to inspect. For each instruction, I ask whether it supplies knowledge the model lacks or protects against a mistake with real consequences.

Over-engineering becomes visible when instructions conflict, several files repeat slightly different versions of the same rule, or a procedure hardcodes details from one customer. To test a suspected redundant instruction, save a copy, remove it, and rerun several familiar tasks, including a case where that rule should matter. One successful output tells you very little. Keep required review rules even when the test cases do not trigger them.

This is also why I keep customer numbers out of reusable procedures. A procedure can tell the agent how to find a renewal date, a contract value, or an acquisition target. The customer's actual values belong in a dated file or a current system result.

Voice dictation can be a useful way to get your thinking down. Before running a long dictated prompt, have the agent organize it. Try this:

> Organize this rough request into the decision I need to make, the relevant sources, the constraints, and the deliverable. Identify conflicting instructions and questions I need to answer. Suggest what to load now and what to retrieve only if needed. Preserve my intent and show the revised request before executing it.

For a familiar task, compare the original and revised prompts using the same source data. Judge the outputs by factual accuracy, missed requirements, and the amount of correction you had to do. A shorter prompt is useful only if it still asks for the work you need.

To follow along, pick one customer and a real question you want to investigate. Use the same customer through the checkpoints below. Your harness is the setup around the model: the files it can retrieve, the tools it can use, and the instructions it follows. An agent with access to a project folder is enough to begin. If a source is unavailable, record what is missing and who can supply it.

## Know: maintain a customer file

For each customer, I keep a plain-text file that I can read and correct. It is a working record, not a transcript archive. A short description at the top helps the agent decide when to open it. Roughly 50 to 150 lines is a useful starting size, though the right length depends on the account.

I organize the file around the business question behind the immediate request. A customer may ask about a product setting while trying to fill regional branch capacity, improve the margin on completed jobs, or win more repeat work. The agent needs enough of that context to make a useful recommendation.

I start with a one-sentence description of the business model and current initiatives. The file then has four sections:

1. The customer's customers: who buys, which partners or branches shape the sale, and where the business makes margin.
2. Stakeholders and unit economics: roles, priorities, success measures, margin, lifetime value, and the cost of acquiring or serving a customer.
3. Verified footprint: products in use, relevant systems, key metrics, data flows, and known measurement gaps.
4. Dated strategy ledger: decisions, links to deliverables, open questions, and the source and date of each material fact.

When a decision changes, I replace the old entry and retain a link to the earlier source if it matters. That gives the agent one current version to use. I label unresolved questions so they cannot quietly become facts in a later draft.

A file like this gives the agent a starting point for a renewal discussion, a performance question, or a strategy proposal. It still has to check what changed since the file was last updated.

### Checkpoint 1: make the customer file retrievable

Give your agent a recent customer email and the notes or records you trust. Use this prompt:

> Draft a short customer file using these sources. Organize it into the customer's customers, stakeholders and unit economics, verified footprint, and dated strategy ledger. Add a source and date to each material fact. Mark missing information as unknown and show conflicting facts for my review. Keep proposed explanations separate from confirmed facts. Show me the file before saving it.

Correct the file, then save it somewhere the agent can retrieve in a new session. Add its location and a one-sentence description to your project's instructions or file index. Start a fresh chat and ask the agent for the customer's current goal and most recent agreed decision, with sources. You should be able to open those sources and confirm both answers. If the agent cannot find the file, fix retrieval before adding more context.

### What to carry into a fresh chat

Start a new chat when the task changes or the conversation has accumulated enough dead ends that it is hard to follow. For work that spans sessions, save a short handoff containing the current goal, verified findings with source links, unresolved questions, and the next step. The new session should open the relevant customer file and procedure itself.

An automatic handoff can help with long tasks, provided it carries that short record forward. Copying the entire conversation also copies its stale assumptions. A throttling warning may reflect a usage limit, so treat it separately from the question of whether the current context is still useful.

## Execute: write procedures for work you repeat

A skill is a written procedure that the agent can load for a particular task. I use skills for work I expect to repeat or work where an omission would be costly. Each one tells the agent what to check before it proposes an answer and what to prepare for my review at the end.

The first skills I would write for a sales team cover four jobs:

* **Find the customer's footprint.** Identify products, teams, locations, systems, and metrics before making a claim about the whole relationship.
* **Build a strategy.** Read the customer file, check the unit economics, and put the proposed roadmap in a document.
* **Prepare deliverables.** Turn the plan into a draft email and any supporting document or working sheet. Leave external sharing to the seller.
* **Diagnose a change.** List possible causes, query the relevant systems, check whether the pattern appears elsewhere, and draft a response with sources.

These procedures should say how to find a number, including the query or tool to use. They should never contain a customer's live number as an example. That is an easy way to make a later draft sound precise and be wrong.

Most sales organizations also have a methodology they want reflected in customer work. I put a few checkable actions in the drafting procedure: identify the stakeholders, connect the recommendation to the customer's stated goal, trace each number to its source, and propose a next step. The full training deck remains available when someone actually needs it.

### Checkpoint 2: save one procedure you can reuse

Choose a workflow connected to your customer question, such as preparing a renewal review or investigating a change in product usage. Ask your agent:

> Draft a reusable procedure for this workflow. State when to use it, which customer context to load, what to check before making a recommendation, and what deliverable to prepare. Use only the tools available in this setup. If a needed source is inaccessible, specify what to ask me for. Include how to check the claims and calculations. Use placeholders for customer details. End with a draft for my review.

Review the steps against how you would do the work yourself. Save the result as a skill or reusable instruction file in the location your agent supports, with a short description of when to load it. In a new session, give the agent a task that should use the procedure. Ask it to identify which procedure it loaded and any checks it could not complete. Revise steps that were skipped or could not be followed.

## Prove: investigate before drafting an explanation

When a customer asks why a result changed, I want the agent to produce possible causes before it settles on one. A drop in sales might reflect demand, a product change, an implementation problem, a measurement gap, or a new constraint on the customer's team. The agent checks the systems and source material that bear on each explanation, drops those the evidence does not support, and looks for the same pattern elsewhere. In Ads, that can mean checking campaign goals, bid targets, conversion signals, and tracking.

The sequence I use is:

1. Read the customer question, the thread, and the current customer file.
2. List plausible causes and the data needed to test each one.
3. Check the relevant systems and source documents. Save the source behind every material claim.
4. Look for the same issue across other products, teams, regions, or accounts where relevant.
5. Draft a short reply and a supporting fix plan. Leave both staged for review.
6. Prepare questions for any partner or customer team that owns a missing piece of the data.
7. Put confirmed, dated findings back into the customer file.

The model chooses which questions to ask and helps interpret the results. Queries and scripts calculate metrics; original records establish what someone said or decided. A query can verify a setting or a reported number. Causal explanations may still need more evidence, so I label those conclusions accordingly.

One quick check is to run the diagnosis again in a fresh chat. Hold the source data, reporting period, and metric definitions constant. The wording may change, but reported numbers and calculations should agree. If they differ, inspect the queries, filters, and source versions. Matching answers still need verification: two runs can repeat the same mistake.

### Make the review manageable

Manually reconstructing every investigation would consume much of the time the agent saves. The procedure should prepare the evidence for review as it works: save queries, calculate figures in a tool, and flag claims with missing or conflicting support. The reviewer can then focus on the recommendation and the facts that could change the customer's decision. Asking the model whether it is confident adds no independent evidence.

### Checkpoint 3: inspect the evidence before the reply

Run your customer question using the file and procedure you just saved. Before accepting a draft, ask:

> Show each material claim you plan to put in the reply, its source and date, and whether it is confirmed, provisional, or unsupported. For numerical claims, include the query or calculation. For a proposed cause, show the evidence that supports it and the alternatives you checked. Identify what remains unknown. Then draft a reply whose wording reflects those limits, and propose updates to the customer file. Leave sending and sharing for my review.

Open the source behind the main recommendation. Recalculate one important number and inspect one alternative explanation. A source that merely mentions the topic is insufficient; it must support the actual claim. If a tool was unavailable, the answer should say what could not be checked. Correct the draft, approve the verified file updates, and run another question through the same workflow. That second run tells you whether the setup is reusable.

### An example from Ads: an underspending campaign

An owner of a multi-location home-services business emailed to ask why a Performance Max campaign was spending so little. I gave an agent a short instruction: “Take a look at the email I received from the owner and diagnose what's going on.” The agent had access to the customer file, the email thread, a procedure for querying ad performance, and live reporting tools. The short prompt worked because those pieces were already in place.

The advertiser ran paired Search and Performance Max campaigns across several metro areas. Completed jobs recorded in its customer relationship management system were sent back to Google Ads as conversion events. These events tell the advertising system which customer actions to optimize toward. In roughly four follow-up prompts, the agent found that the affected campaign's automated bidding used web quote requests as its success signal. Completed jobs and calls were recorded but excluded from its bidding goals. Web quote requests had nearly stopped firing. Daily spend had fallen to about a third of its earlier level.

The account-wide check showed a sharp drop in web form conversions across multiple campaigns. That raised the possibility of a site tracking change. The available data ruled out budget as the immediate constraint. The agent did not claim that competition was responsible because it had not queried competition data.

The same goal configuration appeared in several other campaigns. The proposed fix added the missing goal without immediately removing existing goals, which could have reduced the conversion volume available to bidding. The agent also caught a separate launch problem: recent completed-job uploads all had zero conversion value. The customer planned to optimize bidding for the value of completed work, so those missing values needed to be resolved first.

The output was a draft reply, a linked fix-plan document, and targeted questions for the partner responsible for the upload pipeline. Each material finding had a data source. I still reviewed the diagnosis and the language before anything went to the customer. In another industry, the systems and metrics would differ. The sequence of checking context, testing explanations, and reviewing the draft would be the same.

### Checks I keep in the Ads diagnostic procedure

* Separate conversions used for bidding from other recorded conversions.
* Check campaign-level goals. They can override account defaults.
* Compare a campaign problem with account-wide patterns before assigning a cause.
* Check upload diagnostics and conversion values when CRM data is involved.
* Account for reporting lag and click-date attribution when discussing recent changes.
* Inspect the effect of removing a goal before recommending that change.
* Keep the query behind each number in the draft or its appendix.

## Match review to the cost of being wrong

Internal plans need sourced numbers and causes labeled as confirmed or provisional. Customer recommendations need current evidence, a claim-to-source list, and human review. Pricing or budget changes, forecasts, policy questions, and competitor claims warrant a second reviewer.

The cost of a plausible but wrong answer is concrete. A customer might change a budget, alter a rollout, or commit to a plan on the strength of it. In my Ads work, a wrong diagnosis could also prompt a bid change or a paused campaign. In a customer-facing role, the company shares the loss of trust. I try to write only claims I could explain with the underlying source open in front of me.

## Clean up a setup you already use

Some errors begin in the agent's instructions or memory. A procedure might contain a sample contract value that the agent later treats as a customer's real number. A screenshot summary might sit in the customer file for months with no date. Both can emerge in a polished draft as apparent facts.

Before changing an established setup, keep a copy of the files and instructions that currently work. Ask the agent to inventory what it can inspect. Depending on the product, that may include project instructions, saved skills, customer files, or visible memory entries. It may have no access to other stored context. The inventory should identify that boundary explicitly.

### Checkpoint 4: audit before archiving

Use this prompt in the setup you want to clean up:

> Inventory the instructions, skills, memory entries, and customer files you can actually inspect. Give their locations and roles. List what you cannot inspect. Flag duplicate or conflicting instructions, superseded facts, undated metrics, and customer details embedded in reusable procedures. For each proposed change, show the evidence and its likely effect. Propose what to keep, update, or archive. Make no changes until I review the proposal.

Check the proposed changes against the source material. Archive superseded content outside the locations loaded by default, retain links where history matters, and update the file index. Make a small batch of changes, then rerun familiar tasks with the same source data. Compare the claims, sources, and required review steps with the earlier outputs. Restore anything whose removal caused a regression. Repeat the audit when instructions accumulate or the agent starts giving oddly specific answers.

To teach this across a team, collect a few resolved customer questions with known source evidence. Have colleagues use the same questions to practice selecting context, running a procedure, and reviewing the draft. Include a case with missing data or an outdated target. Compare unsupported claims and missed checks, then improve the shared procedure. A seller should finish the exercise able to explain where the main recommendation came from.

After the first few runs, review where you still have to intervene. A missing fact may belong in the customer file. A check the agent repeatedly skips may belong in the procedure. A conclusion that nobody can verify may require access to another source or a narrower recommendation. Use those failures to decide what to change next.

The home-services case changed my standard for a useful agent workflow. The short prompt was the least interesting part. The value came from current customer context, a repeatable investigation, and a draft whose important claims I could check before the customer saw it. That standard applies just as well to a renewal plan or a question about product adoption.
